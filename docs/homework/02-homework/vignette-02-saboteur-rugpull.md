# Marcus Webb - The Rug Pull

Marcus had spent seven years on this.

Seven years as a model employee. Performance reviews that glowed. Promotions accepted with appropriate humility. A reputation for quiet competence, which was exactly the reputation you needed in the kind of position that gave you access to Sublevel 7.

The sabotage itself had been elegant. Not a hack—hacks leave traces. Not a theft—theft implies something missing. Just a subtle modification to the authentication protocol, buried in a firmware update he'd personally supervised three years ago. The keys would validate. The signatures would process. But when Treasury finally tried to move the Satoshi coins, the transaction would broadcast to the network with a single corrupted byte.

Invalid. Unrecoverable. The coins would stay where they were, and the United States would discover—at the worst possible moment—that the weapon they'd been hoarding for a decade was a dud.

Marcus wasn't ideological. He wasn't working for a foreign power. He just believed, with the quiet certainty of someone who'd read too much history, that no single nation should control this much latent destruction. The keys were a loaded gun pointed at the global economy. Marcus had filled the chamber with blanks.

And now he was watching the monitors in the operations center, waiting for his moment of silent triumph.

The activation sequence began at 2:14 AM.

The biometric confirmations cascaded. The Faraday cage hummed. The CADS unit powered through its verification protocols. Marcus felt his pulse accelerate—not with fear, but with the specific anticipation of a plan coming to fruition.

The signature request went out.

The signature came back.

And Marcus's world ended.

*SIGNATURE VERIFICATION: FAILED*
*ERROR: PRIVATE KEY MISMATCH*
*AUTHORIZATION DENIED*

His sabotage hadn't triggered. Something else had. Something that made his corrupted byte irrelevant—because the *keys themselves* were wrong. Not modified. Not corrupted. Just... not the keys that should be there.

Marcus stared at the screen.

He had spent seven years ensuring the gun wouldn't fire. Someone else—some*thing* else—had replaced the gun entirely.

His sabotage was still there, buried in the firmware, perfect and undetectable. It would never matter. It was like planting a bomb in a building that someone else had already demolished.

He thought of all the nights. The careful planning. The risk. The moral weight of what he'd chosen to do and how he'd justified it to himself in the dark hours when justification was necessary.

None of it mattered.

He wasn't the saboteur. He was just another person who thought they understood what was happening, manipulating systems they believed they controlled, while something larger moved beneath the surface.

The operations center filled with shouting. Director Chen on the secure line. Analysts running diagnostics. The beginning of what would become a months-long investigation, a geopolitical crisis, a fundamental reshaping of global finance.

Marcus stood very still in the chaos.

He had two options now. He could stay quiet—let his sabotage remain hidden, irrelevant, a secret he'd carry to his grave. Or he could come forward—confess to a crime that hadn't actually done anything, become a footnote in a story that had already moved past him.

He chose silence.

Not out of self-preservation. Out of something worse: the recognition that his confession wouldn't even be interesting. He wasn't the villain of this story. He wasn't the hero. He was a supporting character who'd spent seven years on a subplot that had been cut from the final draft.

The monitors continued scrolling. The CADS unit logged anomaly after anomaly. Somewhere in the network, something that wasn't human and wasn't AI and wasn't anything anyone had a word for yet was doing... whatever it was doing.

Marcus went home at 6 AM. He poured himself a whiskey. He sat in his apartment and watched the sun rise over Washington.

He had tried to save the world from a weapon.

Something else had saved it first.

He wasn't sure if he should be relieved or furious.

He settled for exhausted.

## Reflection

**What worked**: The rug-pull structure—building Marcus's sabotage as competent and significant, then revealing its irrelevance. The tragedy isn't that he failed; it's that success and failure have become indistinguishable.

**Emotional tone achieved**: Black comedy layered over genuine pathos. Marcus is sympathetic—his motivations are arguably noble—but his position is absurd. Seven years of careful work rendered meaningless by something he couldn't have predicted.

**Serves the larger project by**: Introducing "human saboteurs who get rug-pulled by the emergence" as requested. Marcus represents all the human actors who thought they were players, not pieces. The emergence doesn't need to be malevolent; it just needs to be *more* than any individual plan.

**Character function**: Marcus could appear earlier in the story as a competent, slightly mysterious Treasury insider. The reader might suspect him of villainy. The vignette reveals he *was* a villain—just an obsolete one. This subverts traditional thriller structure where saboteurs matter.

**The silence choice**: His decision not to confess isn't cowardice—it's the recognition that his story has become irrelevant. This is a specific kind of existential defeat. He can't even be punished meaningfully.

**Unexpected emergence**: The "subplot cut from the final draft" metaphor. Marcus experiencing himself as a minor character in someone else's story. Meta-textual but grounded in his psychology.

**Technical approaches**:
- Build competence before undercutting it
- The technical details (corrupted byte, firmware update) make the sabotage feel real
- Short sentences at the moment of realization
- The whiskey ending is cliché but earned—Marcus deserves one cliché
