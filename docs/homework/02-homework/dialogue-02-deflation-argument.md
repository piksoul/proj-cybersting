# The Deflation Argument

## Setting

A converted warehouse apartment in Rotterdam-Zuid. Grey light through industrial windows. Coffee that's been sitting too long. MIRA OKONKWO, mid-50s, former World Bank, now "consulting" for interests she won't name, sits across from ELIAS at a table covered in printouts.

---

**MIRA**: You're thinking about this wrong. Everyone does.

**ELIAS**: I'm thinking about it the way three semesters of macroeconomics taught me to think about it.

**MIRA**: Exactly my point.

*(She pulls a printout from the stack—a price index chart spanning 200 years)*

**MIRA**: What do you see?

**ELIAS**: Inflation. Gradual, then accelerating after 1971.

**MIRA**: Before that. The nineteenth century. The long deflation.

**ELIAS**: Prices fell. The textbooks call it—

**MIRA**: —The "Long Depression." Yes. Prices fell for decades. Steadily. Predictably. And what happened?

**ELIAS**: Economic stagnation. Reduced investment. The usual deflationary spiral.

**MIRA**: *(leaning back)* Living standards tripled. Industrial output increased eight-fold. The working class gained purchasing power for the first time in history. That's your "spiral."

**ELIAS**: You're cherry-picking. There were also bank panics, unemployment—

**MIRA**: There are always bank panics. The question is: who benefits from the panic? In a deflationary environment, savers benefit. The working class. Anyone holding currency. In an inflationary environment—

**ELIAS**: —debtors benefit. Borrowers. People with leverage.

**MIRA**: And who has leverage?

*(Silence. Elias looks at the chart differently now.)*

**MIRA**: Every central banker, every Treasury official, every tenured economist—they all tell the same story. Deflation is dangerous. Deflation must be prevented at all costs. Ask yourself: dangerous to whom?

**ELIAS**: You're describing a conspiracy.

**MIRA**: I'm describing an incentive structure. The people who set policy are the people who benefit from inflation. It's not a conspiracy. It's just... *(gestures vaguely)* ...selection pressure. The deflationist economists don't get the jobs.

**ELIAS**: So—what? Technology should make everything cheaper?

**MIRA**: Doesn't it? Your phone. Your television. Computing power. Storage. Every year, more capability for less money. That's the natural direction. Productivity gains should flow to consumers as lower prices. Instead— *(taps the chart)* —the gains are captured. Siphoned off through monetary expansion. You work harder, produce more, and your purchasing power stays flat. The difference goes... somewhere else.

**ELIAS**: To asset holders.

**MIRA**: To asset holders. To debtors. To anyone positioned to benefit from currency debasement. The working class runs on a treadmill that speeds up every year, and they're told it's for their own good.

*(She finishes her cold coffee)*

**MIRA**: Bitcoin doesn't inflate. By design. And the entire monetary establishment treats this as a flaw rather than a feature. Ask yourself why they're so afraid of money that can't be printed.

**ELIAS**: Because it would— *(stops himself)*

**MIRA**: Yes?

**ELIAS**: It would break their control mechanism.

**MIRA**: *(smiling, not kindly)* Now you're thinking about it correctly.

## Reflection

**What worked**: The dialogue format makes abstract economic theory feel like genuine intellectual conflict. Mira doesn't lecture—she leads Elias to conclusions through questions, which is more engaging than exposition.

**Emotional tone achieved**: The unsettling feeling of having assumptions questioned. Elias (and the reader) started with "common knowledge" and ended uncertain. Mira's final smile carries an edge—she might be right, but she's also clearly ideological.

**Serves the larger project by**: Introducing the deflation argument without stopping the narrative for an essay. The disagreement feels real because neither character fully "wins." Elias is learning but not converted—appropriate for where he is in the story.

**Key dialogue techniques**:
- Questions rather than statements from the teacher figure
- Specific data (200-year chart, 19th century) rather than vague claims
- The student interrupting, showing genuine engagement
- Physical actions (coffee, printouts) grounding the intellectual content
- The final exchange where the student completes the teacher's thought

**Cherry-picked complexity topics this could model**:
- The 20,000 Satoshi wallets (through similar data-driven dialogue)
- Proof-of-work scaling (through similar question-and-answer structure)
- The emergence (through similar "ask yourself why" Socratic method)

**Character note**: Mira works as a foil because she's not heroic—she has her own agenda, her own ideology. She's useful to Elias but not trustworthy. This ambiguity is important for the story's paranoid tone.
