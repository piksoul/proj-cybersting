# Elias Leaves Treasury - Dawn Exit

The elevator deposits him into the lobby and the morning light hits like an accusation.

Six hours underground. He'd forgotten about daylight. About the way marble floors reflect government fluorescents, about security guards who say "Have a good day, sir" without looking up from their screens.

Have a good day.

The revolving door pushes him onto 15th Street and the cold air fills his lungs with something that isn't recycled nitrogen and copper-tinged coolant. Cars pass. A cyclist. A woman walking a small dog that strains against its leash toward a fire hydrant.

All of this was happening while he was learning that the foundation of global finance was built on compromised code.

The dog barks at something Elias can't see.

He walks. He doesn't remember deciding to walk but his feet are carrying him north toward the Mall, toward open space, away from the building that suddenly feels like it might swallow him back if he stands still too long.

A coffee cart. The vendor, early sixties, wool cap, breath fogging in the November air. "What can I get you?"

Elias stares at the menu board. Americano. Latte. Cappuccino. These words. These ordinary words. He ordered coffee yesterday. He was a different person yesterday. He was someone who believed in verification protocols.

"Sir?"

"Black. Large."

The transaction happens. Money changes hands. Steam rises from a paper cup. The vendor has a Nationals bumper sticker on his cart. Someone cares about baseball. Someone wakes up at 5 AM to sell coffee and cares about baseball and doesn't know that the Satoshi keys are compromised.

Elias walks toward the Washington Monument. It rises in the distance, white against gray sky, the most surveilled piece of real estate in America. Cameras everywhere. Tracking everything. Missing nothing.

Missing the same thing they'd all missed for fourteen years.

A jogger passes. Earbuds in. Tracking her own data. Heart rate, steps, route—feeding it all to servers somewhere, trusting the infrastructure, trusting the verification.

Elias's phone buzzes. Work email. Chen, probably. He doesn't look.

The coffee burns his tongue and he doesn't mind. Something real. Something with consequence he can feel. Not like the numbers on his screen, abstract sequences that mean nothing until suddenly they mean everything, until suddenly they mean the world's reserve currency might be sitting on a trap door nobody noticed for fourteen years.

He sits on a bench. The Monument watches. The cameras watch. Somewhere in the Treasury building, data centers hum and verification protocols run and every single one of them will return green because green is what they're designed to return.

He should call someone. He should tell someone. He should—

Who?

Who do you tell when the people who built the system are the ones the system reports to?

His coffee goes cold. The sun rises higher. Tourists begin to appear, taking photos of monuments they don't really see.

Elias sees everything now.

That's the problem.
