## The Scene

The climbing gym was empty at this hour—Leon had made sure of that. Something about knowing the owner, about favors owed and repaid. Elias didn't ask. He was learning not to ask about Leon's network of debts and obligations.

"Again," Leon said.

Elias stared up at the wall. Fifteen meters of artificial rock, holds mapped in a pattern that should have been easy. Should have been. His arms were burning, his fingers were raw, and he'd fallen six times in the last hour.

"I can see the route," he said. "I just can't—"

"You're thinking too much."

"That's what I do. That's why you hired me."

"I hired you to see patterns. Seeing and thinking aren't the same thing." Leon handed him the chalk bag. "Your body knows the route. Your brain is getting in the way."

Elias chalked his hands, feeling the powder dry against his torn skin. The wall loomed above him, indifferent, waiting.

"What does this have to do with the job?"

"Everything." Leon settled against the crash pad, watching. "When we're in the field, you're going to see the pattern. The timing, the angles, the path through. And you're going to want to control it—every variable, every contingency. You're going to try to think your way through instead of moving your way through."

"And that's bad?"

"That's fatal. The pattern's already there. Your job isn't to build it—it's to follow it. Trust what you see and move."

Elias grabbed the first hold. Pulled himself up. The second hold was where he always stalled—a bad angle, requiring a reach that felt wrong, that his mind screamed was impossible even though he knew it wasn't.

"Don't stop," Leon said. "The moment you stop, you start thinking. Keep moving."

He reached. Felt his fingers touch plastic. Slipped.

The fall was short—barely two meters—but the impact still knocked the wind out of him. He lay on the crash pad, staring at the ceiling, wondering why he'd agreed to any of this.

"Again."

"I'm tired, Leon."

"I know. Do it anyway."

Something in Leon's voice made him sit up. Not command, exactly. Understanding. The voice of someone who'd been here before, fallen here before, gotten up and climbed again.

"Why do you care?" Elias asked. "About me. About any of us."

Leon was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was different—softer, older.

"Because I used to be you. Quick mind, no trust. I thought I could outthink every problem, and when I couldn't, I blamed the problem instead of myself." He shrugged. "Took me twenty years to learn that the problem wasn't my plans. The problem was that I couldn't stop planning long enough to act."

"What changed?"

"I met people who were better at trusting than I was. Watched them move through situations I would have frozen in. And eventually—eventually—I learned to trust them. Then I learned to trust myself."

Elias looked at the wall. The route was still there, unchanged. The holds hadn't moved.

"You think I can learn that?"

"I think you're already learning it. You just don't know it yet."

He stood. Chalked his hands again. The route was the route—fifteen meters, eight difficult moves, one impossible reach that wasn't impossible at all.

*Don't think. Move.*

He grabbed the first hold.

This time, when he hit the bad angle, he didn't stop. His body reached before his mind could object, fingers finding plastic, weight shifting, momentum carrying him past the point where he usually stalled.

Second hold. Third. Fourth.

He was climbing without thinking, his pattern-sight feeding him the next move before he'd finished the current one. Not controlling—following. The route existed. He was just moving through it.

Eight. Nine. Ten.

His arms screamed. His lungs burned. He could feel the crash pad waiting below, patient as gravity.

Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen.

The top.

He slapped the final hold, hung there for a second, then let go. The fall was longer this time—fifteen meters of controlled descent into the pad below—and when he landed, his whole body rang with exhaustion.

Leon was smiling.

"You felt it," he said. "The moment you stopped fighting."

Elias lay on the pad, breathing hard. "I felt it."

"That's what the job is going to be. Seeing the pattern. Trusting the crew. Moving through instead of trying to control."

"And if I fall?"

"Then you fall. But you won't fall because you thought too much. You'll fall because something genuinely went wrong—and that's a better kind of falling." Leon offered him a hand, pulled him up. "Same time tomorrow?"

Elias looked at the wall. At the route he'd finally climbed.

"Same time tomorrow."

## Notes After Writing

**What worked**:
- The climbing metaphor maps directly to the job
- "Seeing and thinking aren't the same thing" as core lesson
- Leon's backstory ("I used to be you") creates mentorship depth
- The successful climb feels earned after six failures
- "A better kind of falling" as practical wisdom

**What needs testing**:
- Is the climbing extended metaphor too on-the-nose?
- Does Leon share enough of himself?
- Should there be more physical detail of the climbing?
- Does the lesson land or feel like a lecture?

**Questions for next draft**:
- Where does this fall in the G-arc? (Training montage section?)
- Should other crew members be present?
- Does this scene need conflict or is teaching enough?
- How does this callback pay off in Precision Sequence?
