# Scene 1 — The Training Space

The freeze held for three seconds before Katya's left arm gave out.

She hit the concrete shoulder-first, rolled, came up already laughing. The other breakers didn't stop — a glancing acknowledgment, a grin, someone's foot finding the beat again. The speaker in the corner pushed something low and repetitive, bass frequencies more felt than heard, and the session continued around her like water around a stone.

Elias watched from the edge of the marked zone, weight balanced, arms loose. Three years and he still stood the same way he had in briefing rooms — reading a space before committing to it. Old habit wearing new clothes.

"You're watching again." Katya pulled chalk from the bag by the wall, worked it into her palms. Didn't look up. "Either join or coach. Standing there is creepy."

"Your left arm is doing all the work."

She looked at him then. The expression she made when she was deciding whether to take instruction or take offence — a half-second of internal negotiation, Serbian and stubborn on both sides.

"Let the hip carry it," he said.

She didn't answer. Moved back to her mark, set herself, and tried again. This time the weight transferred cleanly, the freeze locked, and she held it for a count of five — arm barely involved, the whole structure balanced through her centre. She came down clean, stood up, and walked back to the chalk bag without a word.

He took that as agreement.

The space had been a light-industrial unit before whatever it was before that — the layers of use were written into the walls. Exposed brick buried under graffiti buried under newer graffiti, the oldest tags geological now, their colours bled into the mortar. The ceiling went up seven metres and got lost in shadow past the reach of the yellow work lights, which Dominik had arranged in a rough perimeter on their tripod stands, the way he arranged everything: efficiently, without discussion, as though comfort were a logistical problem he'd already solved.

Cold outside. It had been cold for weeks, the kind of Berlin November that came in like a landlord. Inside, the heat was bodies and movement, and the concrete under Elias's bare palms when he moved was cold anyway, always cold, and he'd stopped noticing.

He moved.

Not performing — working. The sequence he'd been refining for two months, a flow through a jump to a precision to a wall-run to a controlled fall. The technical vocabulary had come slow, learned by repetition and failure and the patient corrections of a man who didn't waste words. The sequence still wasn't right. The jump-to-precision had a half-beat of hesitation in it that he couldn't train out, some failure of commitment that showed up in the landing angle. He knew where it came from. He wasn't ready to fix it.

What happened when you moved well was that everything else stopped. That was the discovery that had kept him here three years past the point where he'd planned to leave — the silence that lived inside the right movement. His mind had always been loud. Pattern after pattern surfacing, connections proliferating, the signal-to-noise ratio of a world that was mostly noise grinding against a brain that couldn't stop processing it. Breaking didn't fix that. It occupied it completely. The body was a system you could actually trust. It gave you real feedback. It didn't lie about what it could and couldn't do.

He'd built three years inside that silence. He was not unaware of what that meant.

Leon was watching from the east wall, back against the brick, arms folded.

He was always watching, which could have been unnerving and somehow wasn't. Forty-five years old and he moved through spaces the way water found level — unhurried, inevitable. He'd been teaching movement for longer than some of the crew had been alive. His own story between Marseille and Berlin was one of those gaps in a person's history that accumulated weight the less it was discussed. Elias had never asked. Leon had never explained. The mutual arrangement suited them both.

He didn't say anything when Elias landed the precision wrong. Didn't say anything when he reset and tried it again, the same half-beat of hesitation in the same place. Just watched. One hand came up briefly and made a gesture — barely a gesture, more a notation — that meant again.

Elias went again.

This time he committed early, shifted his weight a quarter-second before the logical moment, gave the landing something to arrive into instead of catching up to. The precision landed clean. He stood, turned, looked at Leon.

Leon nodded once. That was the whole conversation.

Across the space, Dominik was drilling a wall-run with the same mechanical patience he brought to everything. Not trying to feel it — trying to encode it. He'd been doing it for forty minutes. The same wall, the same approach, the same angular exit. He would get it by repetition before he got it by instinct, and probably he'd never get it by instinct, and this was fine by Dominik, who trusted repetition more than inspiration. The result was a movement that looked precise rather than fluid, efficient rather than beautiful. It worked.

He was the one who kept them in this space — some arrangement with a building owner who owed him something, or who he owed something, depending on which version of the story Dominik was telling. The utilities ran, the locks worked, the space stayed theirs. Elias had never paid for it in anything but presence and didn't examine the economics further.

Katya tried the freeze again. Nailed it. Held it two seconds longer than necessary, which was the only celebration she allowed herself. Then she was already moving, already working the next sequence, her dark hair loose and catching the light as she moved through a combination that shouldn't have worked as cleanly as it did.

She was twenty-three. She'd arrived from Novi Sad with a competition record and a wariness that had taken six months to relax into something approaching trust. The wariness hadn't left — it had just redistributed itself, pointing outward instead of inward. She pushed everyone, herself hardest. She didn't look for protection. Elias had made the mistake of offering it, early on, and she'd explained his error in terms he hadn't required explaining twice.

The space was theirs in the way that only spaces made by chosen presence could be. Not owned. Occupied by will and return and the accumulation of evenings like this one, which had layered into something that functioned like permanence without making any promises.

He hadn't expected to have anything like this again.

He tried not to examine it too directly, the way you didn't look straight at something you were afraid might not be real.

The session wound down by friction rather than decision. People peeled off in twos and threes — conversations finishing, bags retrieved, layers of clothing reconstructed for the cold outside. There was a quality of warmth in the departure: the ease of people who would be back, who didn't need to make it into anything. Brief exchanges, contact, the particular social grammar of a group that had become fluent in each other.

Dominik left with three others, his voice dropping into a different register as he walked — the event-promoter voice, smooth and transactional, already managing whatever came next. Katya gave Elias a look on her way out that contained approximately equal parts affection and warning, the Serbian economy of expression that had taken him two months to read correctly.

The space got quieter. The speaker cycled into something slower, something without percussion, that hung in the air like a question.

Elias sat with his back against the west wall, forearms on his knees. The chalk on his hands was drying to a grey film. He looked at the marks on the concrete floor where years of practice had worn the landing zones distinct from the surrounding surface — subtle, unintentional, accurate as any graph. Evidence of what happened here, compressed into the floor.

Leon lowered himself down beside him, neither close nor far. He'd brought two cups of something from the kettle in the corner, set one within reach without comment.

The question Elias hadn't asked — not aloud, not to himself with any precision — was whether he'd earned any of this. The space, the crew, the evenings, the silence that lived in good movement. The life assembled from what was left over after the original one had burned. He'd rebuilt something. Small. Real. The question wasn't whether it was good. The question was whether a man who'd run as far and as fast as he had, from as much, was owed good things in the place where he'd stopped running.

He didn't examine that either.

The bass moved through the floor, faint now. Chalk dust hung in the work lights' yellow reach, slowly settling. The cold of the concrete was in his palms. Outside, Berlin was wet and lit and watching everything the way it always was — ambient, ancient, patient as geology.

He picked up the cup Leon had left him. Drank.

The silence between them had the quality of something that didn't need filling.
