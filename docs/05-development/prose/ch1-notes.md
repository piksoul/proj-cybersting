Scene 1 — The Training Space
The kick landed first, always the kick, and then the snare pulled everything after it into shape. Elias came down off the wall and reset at the edge of the cipher, palms cold from the concrete, shoulder quiet for now, and listened to the track settle under the movement in the room. An old funk break. Deep rotation — Leon's foundational hour, the crate-dug stuff he put on when the session still had work left in it. The bass came up through the floor more than through the air.
He ran the sequence again in his head before running it in his body. Jump to precision to wall-run to controlled fall. Shoulder-friendly by design, and he'd designed it knowing why, even if he didn't say the reason aloud to anyone. The precision was the problem. Had been for a week. There was a half-beat of hesitation in the commitment — something that lived between the peak of the jump and the landing, a slight drawing-back that had nothing to do with what his mind wanted and everything to do with what the joint remembered. The body protecting the joint without asking first.
He ran it anyway. The jump was clean. The hesitation was there, a familiar ghost in the mechanism, and then the precision didn't stick and he came down half a foot wide of where he'd planned and absorbed it through the hip instead of the landing. Not a failure. Not a success. The same unfinished thing it had been yesterday.
Katya was across the space attempting a freeze on the far side of the cipher. He watched without making it obvious he was watching. She loaded the weight, tried to find the balance point, and the arm gave out and she hit the concrete shoulder-first with a sound like a book dropped flat. She rolled up immediately, the way she always did, already laughing at herself. Walked to her water bottle, took one sharp sip, set it down with the precision of someone resetting a clock. Went back to the same spot. Loaded the same weight. Found the balance point this time and held it — two seconds, three — and then walked away without looking to see if anyone had seen.
Elias looked away before she could catch him looking.
Along the east wall, Leon was deep in conversation with the Glasgow blow-in, hands moving as he talked, making some point about mechanics or philosophy or both at once, because with Leon they were often the same point. The blow-in was nodding along with the serious expression of someone trying to keep up. Leon didn't look at Elias while Elias was running the sequence. He looked at Elias while talking to someone else, in the way of someone who has been watching movement for thirty years and doesn't need to stop other things to do it. Elias caught the glance on the second pass and looked away. A few minutes later, across the space, without breaking stride from whatever he was saying: Half-beat late on the precision, Elias. Same one as yesterday. And then back to the blow-in as if nothing had been said.
Dominik was running a wall-run on the north side with the patient mechanical repetition he applied to everything — the same approach he used to arrange the yellow work lights, which stood on their tripod stands in a rough perimeter casting the space into useful light and useful shadow, ceiling lost seven metres up in the dark. Dominik drilled movement the way he drilled logistics: encode it through enough repetition that the thinking part became unnecessary. Felix had inserted himself into Dominik's orbit twenty minutes ago and was still there, wiping his face with the back of his hand, offering enthusiastic commentary on each attempt. Dominik's responses were technically present and wholly evacuated of interest, the conversational equivalent of a door left slightly ajar that you were not actually being invited to come through.
Papa arrived somewhere in the middle of the session, the door at the far end admitting a brief blade of cold before it closed, and moved through the room doing his round. The Bhutanese pair — cousins, serious, the kind of quiet that comes from having nothing to prove — got the full dap, the handshake that went through three stages and ended in the finger-flick follow-through that Papa delivered to everyone with the same total attention, as if each one was the first of the evening. The Glasgow blow-in got a fist bump. Katya got a fist bump mid-sequence and returned it without stopping, her other hand already moving. When Papa reached Elias, he put a hand on his shoulder instead. Not the ritual. Just the hand, briefly, and then he moved on. He didn't say anything. He never did. Elias didn't examine it. He never did that either.
Felix tried to engage Papa in conversation on the way past and got a fist bump and a nod that managed to convey warmth while closing every possible avenue of continuation.
The Bhutanese pair were working power moves in the far corner — one of them trying to chain a windmill into a freeze and stalling out every time on the transition, the other watching with the focused quiet of someone logging data. The Polish dancer was doing top-rock in a slow private loop near the speaker, completely self-contained. The Japanese exchange student was somewhere in the middle of the space drilling something Elias hadn't seen her attempt before, each attempt precise and tight and slightly wrong in the same way each time, which meant she was close to whatever she was looking for and knew it.
He reset at the edge of the cipher. Sip from the bottle. The concrete was cold under his palms when he pressed them down — November finding its way through the floor even with the bodies and the movement generating heat. Cold outside like a landlord, warm inside from work. The shoulder was a low hum now. Third or fourth pass through the sequence and it had loosened into something that lived in the background rather than the foreground. That was how it worked when the session was going right. The body settled into its own logic and the joint quieted before the mind had registered what was happening.
He ran the sequence. Jumped. The commitment landed early — half a beat before his plan — and something in the jump made the decision for him, something that came from the hip and the accumulated run-up, and the precision was there, weight transferring clean through the centre, and the track on the speaker at that exact moment shifted and Rakim's voice dropped in on top of the kick: Don't Sweat the Technique. He was already in the wall-run, palms flat against the brick, controlled fall coming down, and the title was doing what it said. He didn't notice it consciously. The body noticed it first.
He came out of the fall and stood. Katya was watching from her position across the cipher with an expression that was not quite a smile.
She said, "You're watching again. Either join or coach — standing there is creepy."
"Your left arm is doing all the work," he said. "Let the hip carry it."
She didn't thank him. She went back to the spot and tried the freeze again, and this time something in the loading shifted and the arm got what it needed from the hip and she held it longer than she'd held anything all evening, walking away without looking back at it.

What happened when he moved well was that everything else stopped. That was the discovery that had kept him here three years past the point where he'd planned to leave — the silence that lived inside a movement that worked. The body was a system he'd learnt to trust. The word learnt mattered. It had not come naturally. It had not come fast. There had been a year when the body had refused him entirely, a morning when he'd woken unable to lift his arm past his collarbone, and three physios had given him three versions of the same wrong answer. Posture. Screen time. Stress. Try these exercises. None of it had touched the thing. Breaking had done what the exercises hadn't. The shoulder was not gone — it was never going to be entirely gone — but the joint had come back most of the way and the rest lived in that stress-responsive territory, the last ten percent that loosened in flow and tightened when other things tightened.
He didn't examine why.

Leon walked to the speaker without announcing anything and the track changed. Dub came down over the room — bass dragged through echo chambers, reverb folding back into itself, patient and dragging. The kind of music that ended a session without saying so. Leon's silent decree. He was already back at the wall, resuming the conversation with the blow-in as if he'd never left it.
Katya's shoulders moved first, a roll backward that was more reflex than response, and she was saying something in Serbian to Leon's back — sharp, conclusive — and Leon's nod acknowledged it without turning around. She was picking up her bag before the sentence was finished, jacket pulled on in two motions, gone through the door inside a minute. The cold came in and went out with her.
The room shed people slowly after that. The Polish dancer first, then the Japanese exchange student, packing her things with the same tightness she brought to her drilling. The Bhutanese pair bowed slightly to Papa on their way out and he returned it with the full weight of attention he gave everything. Dominik left with three of the regulars, his voice already dropping into a different register — smoother, more deliberate, the event-promoter frequency he switched to without apparent thought. Felix made one last approach to Dominik's orbit and was redirected with a practiced warmth that contained nothing. Papa did his exit round, shorter than the arrival round but just as complete, and was the last one through the door other than the two of them.
The dub continued. Elias sat at the edge of the cipher with his back against the wall, palms resting loose on his knees, and listened to the bass move through the space. The yellow work lights held their perimeter. The graffiti on the exposed brick went back in layers — the newest tags bright, the older ones fading into the mortar, the oldest almost geological, their colours bled into the wall itself over years of accumulation.
Leon crossed the space and sat against the opposite wall, stretched his legs out, and said nothing. The dub filled the room around them and the session was over.

Scene 2 — After
The space was bigger without the crew in it. That was always how it felt — not empty exactly, but expanded, as if the room exhaled when the bodies left. The yellow work lights were down to one strip near the door, the rest killed. The mats were stacked. Two water bottles sat on the floor between them and neither had been touched since the session ended.
Leon stretched in silence along the opposite wall, slow and deliberate, working through the same routine Elias had watched him work through a hundred times. A body that knew its own maintenance schedule. Elias sat with his legs extended, back against the concrete, and rolled the shoulder once — not because it needed it, just the habit of checking in. The joint answered with nothing in particular. Fine. Better than fine.
Neither of them was in a hurry to leave.
After a while Leon said, without looking up from the stretch: "You forced the precision the first six passes. The seventh you stopped and it came."
"Inspiring lesson."
Leon made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. He moved to the next stretch, unhurried.
Elias looked at the wall. The graffiti layers caught the low light differently now — depth where there'd been surface before, the older tags emerging slightly as if the geological record showed better in the quiet. He'd stared at these walls for three years and they were still giving him new information.
Leon never asked where Elias had come from. That was the rule between them — not stated, not negotiated, just present from the first session and maintained without effort since. You showed up. You trained. You earned your place through the work. No clearances. No past. No credentials except what you could do in the cipher when it was your turn.
It was the best rule Elias had encountered in thirty-six years.
He wondered sometimes if Leon had arrived at the rule from his own direction. Twenty years in Berlin and the story had a gap in it — something between Marseille and here that nobody in the crew could fill in and nobody tried to. The dub had been one of those gaps for six months. Elias had assumed it was ambience, end-of-session atmosphere, until he'd noticed the pattern: track changes, session ends. Simple as a switch. It had taken him that long to read it, which meant Leon had been watching him not read it for six months, and had never pointed it out.
That told you something.
The building settled around them — a low tick from the roof, the shift of old materials adjusting to the temperature drop outside. Through the cracked window near the ceiling came the city at distance: tram rattle, a bicycle bell from somewhere, the sub-bass of a club several streets over arriving more as pressure than sound. Berlin doing what Berlin did when you weren't in the middle of it.
Leon stood. Pulled his jacket from the wall without drama. "Thursday."
Not a question.
Elias nodded.
Leon walked out without looking back. The door closed behind him, and the echo of it moved through the space and settled. The dub ran another few bars in the silence he'd left, bass folding back into itself, patient and uninstructed.
Elias sat for another minute.
Then he got up, pulled on his jacket, and followed.
