Scene 3 — Home

The cold hit the shoulder first.

He'd expected it — the training space held heat from bodies and he'd stayed past the point when the heat thinned — but the joint still registered the drop with a precise, unhappy clench as he pushed through the door into the street. He rolled it once. The ache spread and subsided, settling back into background.

Wet cobblestones caught the sodium light in pools. He walked the route he knew without deciding to, hands in pockets, the grocery bag weight shifting against his thigh. A tram passed at the far end of the street, its windows lit orange, gone before he could watch it go. Somewhere behind the buildings a club was happening — he felt it in the chest more than heard it, the low pressure of sub-bass through several walls of concrete.

He checked his reflection in a darkened shopfront window. Nobody behind him. Old habit. He didn't decide to do it; his eyes moved there anyway, the way the shoulder rolled — the body running its background processes without consulting him. Three years and it hadn't stopped. He wasn't sure he wanted it to.

The corner shop was still open, warm yellow interior bleeding out onto the pavement, steam on the inside of the glass. He pushed in. The owner looked up from his phone with the recognition of a man who had catalogued your schedule without meaning to.

"Late again, my friend." He was already reaching for the shelf. "One day you tell me what you do at night."

"I dance," Elias said.

The owner laughed — a short, genuine sound — and set the tea on the counter. Elias paid. The owner was already back to his phone. Nothing in the exchange that needed examination.

Outside, he walked the last two streets with the takeaway coffee warm through his glove and the tea in the bag and his breath visible in the air ahead of him. Security cameras on every third building — he clocked them without trying, the count automated and unwanted. His digital shadow was thin after three years of careful living, but thin wasn't gone. He knew that better than most. You couldn't outrun the data you'd already generated. You could only stop generating more and hope the old trail went cold before someone followed it to where you were standing.

The shoulder had loosened by the time he reached his building. Almost fully. Almost.

He climbed the stairs slowly. Inside, sparse and deliberate — nothing on the walls, the bookshelf with its small collection of things he hadn't been able to leave behind, the kitchen counter clean. He set the coffee down. Set the tea beside it. Stood there for a moment in the quiet of a man who had arranged his life to require very little of him.

The phone sat on the counter where he'd left it that morning. Dark. He hadn't checked it in weeks. He didn't check it now. It sat there with the patience of something that knew how to wait — beside the tea, beside the coffee going cold, in the ordinary light of a kitchen that didn't know anything was coming.

He left it there and went to bed.
